Valley geography:

Wyrmrock rises above Dartmoor. Once climbing to the peak, you reach a massive lake which collects rain clouds into itself, and flows down the mountain, into the river Calder. The valley is formed into the southeastern slope of Wyrmrock, and in the basin sits Drackhill City, divided into the poverty-stricken Redbridge, the post-industrial rusted Belson, and the wealthy commercial centre of Caldermouth. To the northeast of the valley is the rural region of Blackmarsh, named for its rich soil. West of the river are the Wyrmwoods, a stretch of forests and hills. South of the Wyrmwoods, downroad from Redbridge, is the modern industrial town of Wickermast. Closer to the coast, where Caldermouth stretches into the sea, you'll find the old market town of Danwich, the wealthy village of Rexton, and the harbour town of Angleport. A short boat's ride away is the Undervalley, a drowned river valley. In Prehistoric times, this was a continuation of the valley's rolling hills; now, the old hilltops form islands: namely Bromney, a fishing town; Hatfell, a tourist spot and maritime centre; and Mercy Isle, a 2-square-mile island off the coast, with a storied history.


History of all landmarks:

Drackhill City
	The overall urban area of the valley, Drackhill City numbers at around 275,000 residents, and covers a roughly 29 square mile area. It is primarily divided into the "Three Towns" of Rebdridge, Belson, and Caldermouth. The River Calder runs through the centre of the city, bisecting it and dividing Redbridge and Belson. Towards the coast, the river deviates into several smaller streams, upon which Caldermouth is built, sitting at the Eastern side of the city, while Redbridge stands to the north and Belson to the south.

	Redbridge
		Founded as a Saxon settlement on the western side of the River Calder. Named for the red, clay-rich soil which was used to construct the famous Drackhill Bridge, a large, red-brick bridge that connects both sides of the Calder. For a while Redbridge was the industrial centre of the city, with its quarries and forges generating wealth for the locals; but as Belson rose to prominence in the 19th century, Redbridge fell to the wayside. By the 1950s, Redbridge underwent a rejuvination project as part of national slum-clearing efforts, including the demolition of the Drackhill Bridge and replacement with a steel one; however, embezzlement of funds led to the district being peppered with shoddy houses that often collapsed. Come the 90s, the district was home to several disparate OCGs, which were eventually defeated and subsumed by the emergent Redbridge Syndicate, which spearheaded a community revitalisation programme. By 2015, Redbridge is still heavily poor, and has the largest immigrant population of the so-called "Three Towns" of Drackhill - however, the efforts of the Syndicate have led to an increase in living standards and community harmony. Redbridgers thus often see themselves as distinct from the rest of the city, and the district council often chafes under central authority and seeks further autonomy. Redbridge has a population of 90,750 - a third of the city's population.

	Belson
		Initially an underdeveloped collection of hamlets and fields, Belson (formerly known as Halford Common) underwent a massive transformation in the Victorian era, when industrial magnate Howard Belson set up a company town in the area. By the 1920s, the Belson Company Town was officially incorporated as the Belson District of Drackhill City. Unofficially, "company rule" continued well into the 50s, when the company dissolved due to bankruptcy. Continuing as an industrial centre until the 80s, when the Neoliberal turn led to much of the district's factories going bust, and an influx of migrants alongside lower living standards led to the rise of the neo-fascist Patriotic League, a skinhead gang that quickly became one of the so-called "Big Three" gangs in Drackhill. By 2015, Belson is the most dangerous place in the valley, it being outright unsafe to walk the streets at night alone. A rivalry between the Patriots and a mafia splinter called the "Gold Tooth Grins" (named for their practice of replacing a full member's canine with a golden tooth, as well as several older members sporting Glasgow Grins from old rivalries) has only intensified the district's violence, and rampant police corruption makes the district distinctly unsafe.
		Perhaps the district's only remaining industry is to be found in Belson Industrial Park, which boasts a number of high-rise buildings home to various specialist industries, chief among them Morland Genetics, a major biotech corporation.
		Belson has the largest number of people in the three districts of the city, numbering 129,000 - 47% of the city population.

	Caldermouth
		The wealthy southern district of the city, Caldermouth is the beating heart of the valley. Home to all major sites of commerce, including multiple shopping centres; Caldermouth also houses the Drackhill Council, which since 1989 has been a unitary authority; while technically part of Devon. Drackhill Council's authority extends over the entire valley; as such, Civic Row, a street in the centre, is home to a wide variety of important governmental buildings; including the Drackhill Valley Police HQ, the local Chamber of Commerce, the Assembly which includes the entire Valley Council and the Mayor's office, and the local Channel One building, which handles radio, television, and internet provision to the entire valley. Drackhill University College provides further and higher educatoin across the valley, along with several affliate colleges throughout the region.
		Caldermouth's life began in the Saxon times, as its position at the confluence of the river Calder's many branches made it a major market town, reinforced with the Valley Roads initiative of 1924 which built highways between Caldermouth and all other settlements in the valley. It has the smallest population in the city area, numbering 55,000 - 20% of the city population.

	The Underhill
		Sensationalist accounts of a secret underground society living underneath Drackhill City should be taken with a grain of salt. However... it is true that the city has a very vast underscape, made up of a diversity of features; a valley that gets flooded as often as Drackhill could not have a large modern city without a vast network of storm drains; many of which can be accessed through poorly-maintained manhole shafts. In the 1950s, a plan to create an underground transit system was scrapped, but the old tubelines and proto-stations still remain, and can be accessed through sealed and abandoned entry points - and with a little jury-rigging, could even have power re-provided. Not to mention a plethora of natural caves created by underground rivers; a network of tunnels first dug in the 11th century for water supply pipe maintenance; a major development craze in the 70s that led to several buildings having at least one basement level, many of which are only a short dig away from the rest of the 'Underhill' - combine such extensive subterreanea with a high population of homeless, desperate people, and it's no surprise that an underground community has formed. Derisevely called "Sewer People" by the surface-dwellers, and often dismissed as an urban legend, the transient Underhill community is hard to find a definite number on - estimates vary widely from less than 300, to over 3,000. What is not contested is that several homeless people who spend the day above ground will seek shelter in the Underhill - but rumours of entire undercity tribes, underground laboratories, and the like, are hopefully the product of overactive imaginations...


Greater Valley Area
	Outside of the city, to the western slopes of the valley, Drackhill has a largely rural spread of settlements, largely based upon branches of the Calder.

	Wickermast
		One of the few valley settlements not based upon the river Calder, Wickermast is home to a massive mining complex, which maintained operations up until the 1960s. Initially founded as a logging camp and later a small farmstead by Saxon settlers, it takes its name from its placement at the top of a hill - Wicc meaning settlement, and Meste meaning hilltop. Now a large industrial centre, Wickermast is a relatively modern town. Since the collapse of the mining industry and the neoliberal turn of the 80s, Wickermast has subsumed Belson's role as the leading manufacturer for the region, in particular known for a large number of auto manufacturers located there. Most famously however, it is the UK headquarters for the Eidolon Corporation, one of the world leaders in military hardware. Around 12,000 people live here, but around 5,000 more people commute daily from the city to the area.

	Blackmarsh
		Named for its incredibly rich soil, Blackmarsh began as a former branch of the River Calder, which dried up over the centuries and revealed lush farmland. Locals quickly capitalised on this, and the area is a major bread basket for the entire region. Human settlement is sparse, with most of the area being used for both crops and animal grazing. Animals tend to be kept in the north and west of the area, where the land is more hilled and uneven; while the southeast is home to wide stretches of stable crops. Most farms are owned by large landowners; smallholding is rare. Around 4,800 people live in the area, mainly employed in the farms or the local market square.

	Danwich
		Named for the old Norse settlements in the locality, Danwich (pronounced Dannitch) is nowadays known primarily for its local attractions, hosting a number of old castles, manor houses, and chapels. It has the lowest population of all notable settlements in the valley, standing at 410 as of the latest census.

	Wyrmwood
		The forests of Wyrmwood are situated on the west side of the Calder, primarily concentrated in the northern hills. Home to a large number of old oak trees, deforestation was ceased in the 11th century by the Baron of Drackhill, the Norman lord of the valley, who turned them into a public game reserve. Thus, Wyrmwood is home to a large population of deer, rabbits, and other fauna. Human settlement is intentionally kept to a minimum - only a few tiny hamlets can be found in the forests, typically towards the more developed south. At most, 70 people live in the area, though censuses have not been taken for some time.

	Wyrmrock
		The mountain at the northwest of the valley, Wyrmrock is an imposing slate hill that towers over the city and towns of Drackhill. At its peak, the Caldermere sources the River Calder, which flows through the valley. It is a popular tourist destination, as from the peak one can see both the whole valley as well as much of the southwest. It does not have any permanent population.

	Angleport
		Named for the relative local oddity of a large Angle settlement in the otherwise Saxon Devon, Angleport is home to the second-largest harbour in the valley. Home to an extensive fishing industry as well as some major shipyards, much of its economy is propped up by the relatively large Royal Navy presence there, which pays local shipwrights handsomely. It is home to the largest naval base in the region, and also operates the ferry service to the Undervalley Isles. As of the latest census, the population was 28,000.

	Rexton
		Sometimes jokingly referred to by locals as "The other other coastal town", Rexton is stereotypically home to the wealthy. Originally home to the manor houses of the Barons of Drackhill, it is now an upmarket, upper-class, genteel village. Locals are typically on the older side, and the average income is the highest in the region, at £100,000 per annum. Historically, the village was isolated from the rest of the valley due to both its placement away from the river and its lack of commercial relevance. Its permanent population is 503 as of the latest census, making it only slightly more populous than Danwich, despite its area being twice as large. During the holiday season, it more than triples in population.

The Undervalley Isles
	In the Ice Age, the valley stretched on far ahead through the Channel. However, rising sea levels resulted in the former rolling hills becoming a broad strip of islands. While most are tiny islets that could at most fit a single neighbourhood and are left uninhabited, the islands of Hatfell, Bromney, and Mercy (also known as Lazarus Isle) are alive with settlement.

	Hatfell Island
		Hatfell's history had for most of it been unremarkable; the local economy was primarily aquaculture, with minor pastoralism and maritime industry. However, after the Second World War and the birth of the tourism industry, Hatfell grew from a primarily rural island to a bustling small town. Sitting at 9 square miles and with a permanent population of around 10,000, Hatfell's primary relationship with the mainland is as a tourist trap, with a service-based economy.

	Bromney Island
		Bromney Island's history is almost identical to Hatfell's, except it never underwent the same post-war boom as its "big sister" did. As such, it also avoided the bust of the 70s, due to its far more reliable economic setup. Bromney's economy is almost entirely propped up by the aquaculture industry; large fish farms off the coast along with whole fleets of fishing ships supply to the entire valley, from gourmet lobster to battered cod. It measures at 6 square miles and has a modest population of around 3,500.

	Mercy Isle
		A short boat ride off the coast, Mercy Isle is a 2.1 square mile island that began as a leper and plague colony in the middle ages. Home to a local branch of the Order of St. Lazarus, the monastery and hospital were famous across the region. During the English Reformation, the local branch officially declared itself to be non-denominational (in truth, this branch had been drifting away from its parent order for a while now), and thus was spared the wrath of both Henrian and Marian persecution. Following the Battle of the Armada, the Order leased parts of the isle to the newly founded Royal Navy, the contract remaining (in modified form) to this day. Now, the isle is home to the old monastery, a Royal Marines base, and finally a small shipyard intended for maintenance and refuelling, which primarily services the HMS Lazarus, a "supercarrier" ship designed to operate as a "floating fortress", hosting a number of aircraft, a contingent of marines along with armoured vehicles, and even a station for maintaining and repairing submarines. Thanks to this lease, the Order is fabulously wealthy.
		The Isle's population is hard to guage, but on a given day, around 1200 people are present on the isle - primarily naval staff and prisoners, with less than 50 Lazarites on the isle.
